# Abstract

The exponential growth of scientific publications in fields such as computer science, artificial intelligence, and natural language processing has made it increasingly difficult for researchers and students to stay current with the literature. This paper presents the AI Research Copilot, a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system designed to retrieve relevant academic papers from a curated corpus, extract key contributions, and synthesize that information into grounded summaries, answers, and literature reviews. The system employs a hybrid retrieval engine combining dense vector search, sparse keyword retrieval (BM25), and cross-encoder re-ranking, with generation handled by a hosted large language model. A Streamlit-based interface allows users to pose natural-language research questions and receive citation-grounded responses. The system is evaluated using the RAGAS framework, measuring faithfulness, context precision, context recall, and answer relevance against a held-out question-answer set and a non-RAG baseline. An optional extension incorporates a Neo4j-based citation graph for graph-augmented retrieval. The project demonstrates that combining structured retrieval with generative AI can substantially reduce the manual effort required for literature review while maintaining traceability to source documents.

# 1. Introduction

The number of scientific publications has expanded significantly over the last several years, particularly in areas of computer science (i.e., AI, machine learning, computer vision, and natural language processing). Every month, thousands of new research articles are published and distributed through academic journals and conferences, as well as a variety of repositories such as arXiv. Although the increase in scientific publications facilitates the growth of scientific knowledge, it also makes it difficult for students and researchers to identify all relevant sources of literature. Identifying high-quality papers and understanding their contributions will typically require the reviewer to read through dozens of papers; this process can take a significant amount of time.
Academic search engines allow researchers to search for papers based on a set of keywords and other criteria; when a researcher conducts a search these results will be presented to him/her. While some newer academic search platforms have begun to provide AI-generated summaries and citation graphs alongside search results, most search engines still surface only titles, abstracts, and metadata, leaving the researcher to read through whatever results they obtain to identify the primary ideas and research trends presented by the papers of interest, and to synthesize insights across multiple papers, which none of these tools do directly. If the researcher is conducting a search as part of a literature review, these activities may become very time-consuming.
Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and large language models (LLMs) have enabled the development of AI systems that allow for the ability to analyze vast amounts of text-based information and subsequently provide users with summarized information. However, LLMs used without any retrieval mechanism generate responses purely from their fixed training data, so they cannot verify claims against current sources and may produce answers that are outdated, incomplete, or unsupported by any citable evidence.
The technique of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is meant to address the issues with generating text in situations where the answer either doesn't exist or is poorly represented in the input. By combining both document retrieval and language generation into one unified approach, relevant documents are retrieved from a database before being used to generate answers based on their context. The current project will develop an AI Research Copilot that retrieves academic research papers to analyze the content within them and generate appropriate summaries or responses for any type of academic or research question.

# 2. Literature Review

Lewis et al. [1] introduced RAG as a technique in the field of knowledge-intensive natural language processing (K-NLP) when it comes to answering questions. Their method is based on the belief that K-NLP can be improved significantly by combining neural retrieval models with state-of-the-art generative language models to produce more reliable responses to academic questions. Their method retrieves one or more documents relevant to a query and conditions the generation model on that retrieved content, so that responses are grounded in real-world data rather than relying solely on the parameters learned during pretraining.
Prior to the widespread adoption of LLMs, automated literature analysis systems primarily relied on extractive summarization techniques and keyword-based search engines, which often failed to capture the nuanced semantic relationships between complex scientific concepts. The foundational architecture of modern generative models, which revolutionized this space, relies heavily on the transformer mechanism introduced by Vaswani et al. [2]. While early AI-driven search tools improved paper recommendations, they lacked the generative capabilities required for direct, context-aware question-answering across multiple documents.
Given the need to merge these generative LLMs with external data sources, several frameworks have been designed to facilitate this process. Among general-purpose orchestration frameworks, LangChain offers broader integration coverage, but LlamaIndex is comparatively better optimized for indexing and retrieval-centric workloads, which makes it more suitable for a document-heavy research assistant. Vector databases are equally crucial to today's information retrieval systems. Traditional libraries like FAISS laid the groundwork for large-scale similarity search [3] and remain faster for pure in-memory search, but they leave persistence, metadata filtering, and multi-user access to be implemented separately. Purpose-built vector databases such as ChromaDB trade some of that raw speed for easier setup and built-in persistence, which is a reasonable trade-off for a locally deployed, single-user research tool but may not scale as well to larger, concurrent, or production-grade deployments.
Edge et al. [4] show that structuring a corpus as a knowledge graph and summarizing over its community structure allows a system to answer broad, query-focused questions that purely vector-based semantic search struggles with, since it captures relational context between documents rather than just pairwise similarity. This project takes a graph-augmented retrieval approach inspired by GraphRAG, using Neo4j as the specific graph database to map citation networks between papers; unlike Edge et al.'s community-summarization method, the implementation here performs citation-graph traversal rather than summarizing over community structure, so it is more accurately described as GraphRAG-inspired than as a direct application of [4]. Neo4j itself is a widely used, mature graph platform, but the choice of Neo4j over alternatives (e.g., NetworkX for a lighter-weight, in-memory graph) is an implementation decision rather than one validated in [4].
Furthermore, the evaluation of RAG systems has become a critical area of research. Frameworks like RAGAS (Retrieval Augmented Generation Assessment) have been introduced to quantitatively evaluate the faithfulness, context precision, context recall, and answer relevance of generated responses, ensuring the LLM is strictly grounded in the retrieved literature [5].
Beyond research-oriented frameworks, several commercial and academic tools already target literature discovery and synthesis. Semantic Scholar's TLDR feature generates single-sentence paper summaries but does not perform cross-paper synthesis or answer free-form questions [7]. Elicit and Consensus use LLMs to answer research questions by pulling from indexed paper databases, closer in spirit to this project, but operate as closed, hosted systems without exposing their retrieval architecture, and neither publishes citation-graph-based retrieval or an open evaluation methodology comparable to RAGAS [8], [9]. This project differs in being an open, locally-inspectable pipeline whose retrieval and evaluation methodology (hybrid dense+sparse retrieval, RAGAS-based scoring) is fully specified rather than a black box.

# 3. Problem Statement

The huge and continuously increasing number of published research papers creates an inefficient process for researchers and students to locate and analyze relevant literature. While traditional academic search engines excel at locating research papers, they fail to provide deep insights, cross-paper comparisons, or emerging trend analyses.
As a result, users typically need to read through multiple papers before understanding the key ideas and contributions to a specific research domain. This can take a significant amount of time and slow the progress of research.
Consequently, an intelligent system is needed that can automatically retrieve relevant research papers, extract their key contributions, and synthesize that information into concise summaries and cross-paper comparisons. With such a system, it would be possible for researchers to navigate the growing body of scientific literature far more efficiently.

# 5. Methodology


## 5.1 Core Deliverable vs. Extensions

The hybrid retrieval and generation pipeline — Docling ingestion, dense retrieval via ChromaDB, sparse retrieval via BM25, Reciprocal Rank Fusion, cross-encoder reranking, Gemini-based generation, and the Streamlit interface — constitutes the core deliverable for this project and is scheduled to be complete and independently demoable by week 6 (Section 6). The Neo4j-based citation graph (GraphRAG) is treated as an extension built on top of this core: it is scoped to be attempted only once the core pipeline is validated, and the project remains complete and gradeable without it. A single-configuration RAGAS evaluation of the core pipeline, together with a non-RAG baseline comparison, is itself part of the core deliverable (Section 6); only a further multi-configuration RAGAS ablation is treated as an additional, optional extension. This ordering is intended to ensure that no single component's delay jeopardizes the project as a whole.

## 5.2 Pipeline Architecture

The proposed system is expected to utilize the Retrieval-Augmented Generation architecture for the analysis of the research papers. The system is targeted for development and testing on a local machine with 8 GB of GPU VRAM, which constrains the size of models that can be run locally and motivates several of the tooling choices described below. This is distinct from system RAM, where the system assumes a minimum of 8 GB for the core pipeline (ChromaDB persistence and query-time retrieval/reranking). If the GraphRAG extension is built, the minimum rises to 16 GB (32 GB recommended) to support Neo4j and ChromaDB maintaining persistent memory and connections concurrently. Docling's models are loaded only during ingestion and unloaded before the query-time components become active, so Docling does not add to this concurrent footprint. First, the research papers will be retrieved from various repositories like arXiv. The PDF files will be processed using Docling, an advanced layout-aware parser, which converts complex multi-column text, tables, and mathematical formulas into structured Markdown. Because Docling preserves heading structure during this conversion, the output Markdown is then parsed to identify and separate standard paper sections (e.g., abstract, introduction, methodology, results) so that downstream chunking and retrieval can be section-aware.
The system operates over a pre-ingested corpus rather than fetching papers live per query: papers are pulled from arXiv in a one-time (or periodically refreshed) batch ingestion step ahead of the demo, respecting arXiv's API rate limits. For the project's demo and evaluation, this corpus will consist of approximately 200 papers drawn from two related subfields, natural language processing and computer vision. To build this corpus, the system uses a snowball sampling method: starting with 3-5 seed papers from each subfield, it employs backward snowballing (from reference lists) and forward snowballing (via the Semantic Scholar API, using exponential backoff on rate-limit responses) to reach a densely connected set of ~200 papers. To prevent exponential explosion from highly-cited seed papers, forward snowballing is capped at 15 new candidate papers per source paper at every hop, with a hard ceiling of 300 total Semantic Scholar API calls for the corpus-construction phase; if the corpus falls short of the ~200-paper target after these limits are reached, backward-only snowballing with an expanded seed set is used as a fallback, typically yielding a corpus of approximately 100-150 papers; because this range sits almost entirely below the 150-paper GraphRAG viability threshold (defined in the PRD, Section 7.1.1, based on the expected in-corpus citation-connectivity fraction rather than paper count alone), invoking this fallback will likely trigger descoping of the GraphRAG extension rather than demonstrating it with a sparse graph. This size is chosen to be large enough to exercise meaningful cross-paper synthesis and to produce a sufficiently connected citation graph for the GraphRAG extension (Section 5.3), while remaining tractable for local storage and embedding computation within the project's timeline. Live arXiv search integration is treated as a stretch goal.
The extracted text will then be segmented into smaller chunks using a section-aware chunking strategy: each identified paper section is further split into size-bounded sub-chunks (target ~250–350 tokens) using boundary-aware splitting that treats fenced math blocks and LaTeX equations as atomic units, ensuring equations are never severed across chunk boundaries, which improves the efficiency of the retrieval process. The text segments will be converted into vector representations using the lightweight nomic-embed-text model and stored in a local ChromaDB vector database. To improve precision, a re-ranking step using bge-reranker-base will evaluate the retrieved chunks before they are sent to the generation model. The system will process the user query by converting the query into a text vector. The text vectors will then be compared with the vectors stored in the database. The system will then retrieve the relevant document segments based on semantic similarity. The retrieved context will be passed to Google's Gemini API (Gemini 2.5 Flash) for response generation, accessed as a hosted service rather than run on local hardware. This decouples the generation workload from the local GPU entirely, so the 8 GB of local VRAM is dedicated solely to the lightweight embedding (nomic-embed-text) and reranking (bge-reranker-base) models, comfortably within budget. Within that local budget, the ingestion-time and query-time stages are also kept sequential rather than concurrent: Docling's layout models are unloaded after ingestion-time parsing completes and before the embedding model is loaded, so that only the embedding and reranking models need to be resident in VRAM at query time. Using a hosted generation model in place of a locally quantized one trades a small amount of added latency and an external API dependency for substantially faster development velocity and access to a stronger model than could realistically be run on local hardware, while the retrieval, embedding, and reranking stack that forms the core of the system's design remains entirely local. A locally hosted alternative such as Llama 3 [6] served via Ollama [10] remains a viable fallback path for a fully offline or production-grade deployment where the API dependency is undesirable.
To fully realize the Hybrid Retrieval Engine, the dense vector search via ChromaDB will run in parallel with a sparse keyword search using the bm25s library integrated through LlamaIndex’s BM25Retriever. The bm25s library leverages sparse matrices to eagerly compute scores, offering significant speed and memory efficiency over older BM25 implementations, which is crucial for local hardware. Because standard BM25 tokenization strips or mangles LaTeX commands and mathematical symbols, sparse keyword search over math-block content is expected to be unreliable, so equation and formula matching relies primarily on dense retrieval rather than BM25. The candidate documents from both engines will be merged using LlamaIndex's QueryFusionRetriever. By applying the Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) algorithm, the retriever seamlessly combines semantic similarity scores with exact keyword matches without requiring manual weight tuning. This unified, deduplicated list is then passed to the bge-reranker-base cross-encoder for absolute relevance sorting before being sent to the Gemini generation model. For the literature-review generation objective specifically, multi-paper synthesis will rely on retrieving top-k chunks per sub-topic across the relevant papers and, where the retrieved set is small enough, passing multiple full papers directly within Gemini 2.5 Flash's roughly 1M-token context window, rather than treating literature-review generation as a single-query question-answering task. Finally, the user interface will be built using Streamlit, providing an interactive web application where researchers can input queries and review generated summaries. To prevent redundant API calls during live demos (e.g., an accidental page refresh), the UI layer caches heavyweight model and connection objects — the embedding model, reranker, database client, and retriever — as application-scoped singletons, and separately caches query results keyed by both the query string and a corpus-version identifier so a corpus refresh invalidates stale answers. Real-time visual exploration of the Neo4j citation graph is an additional UI feature, available only if the GraphRAG extension (Section 5.3) is completed; the core UI does not depend on it.
Several failure modes are anticipated and handled explicitly rather than left implicit. If Docling fails to parse a PDF (e.g., a scanned image-only paper with no extractable text layer), an OCR fallback (Tesseract, integrated via Docling's do_ocr option) is attempted first; only papers whose OCR-extracted text still falls below a minimum quality threshold (fewer than 100 extractable words, or a Tesseract mean-confidence score below 60%) are flagged and excluded from the corpus, rather than every unparseable PDF being excluded outright. If the fusion-and-rerank stage returns no chunks above a minimum relevance threshold for a query, the system surfaces a "no sufficiently relevant context found" message rather than forwarding an empty or noisy context to Gemini, reducing the risk of ungrounded hallucination on out-of-corpus questions. Cases where Gemini's response is not fully grounded despite reasonable retrieved context are still expected to occur at some rate; this is precisely what the faithfulness metric in Section 5.4 is designed to quantify, rather than something the pipeline is assumed to eliminate.

## 5.3 GraphRAG Extension


## This section covers the Neo4j-based citation graph, treated as an extension per Section 5.1 above (the corresponding extension scope item in the PRD is Section 5.2, "In Scope (Extension)"). The system will utilize Neo4j Community Edition as a graph database to map citation relationships within the ingested corpus. The citation graph is built primarily from structured reference data retrieved via the Semantic Scholar API during corpus construction, matching against the ingested corpus by ID rather than string. Fuzzy title/DOI matching against Docling-extracted reference text is used only as a fallback for papers Semantic Scholar does not cover. This will enable the system to recognize related papers and highlight important research works within the ingested set. Rather than operating as a fully separate lookup, the graph traversal results are designed to be added as an additional, optional candidate source into the same fusion stage described in Section 5.2. Papers surfaced through graph traversal contribute their existing ChromaDB chunks to the candidate pool, rather than triggering a fresh per-paper retrieval.


## 5.4 Evaluation Plan

To evaluate the system as stated in the objectives, a held-out test set of approximately 30–50 question-answer pairs will be assembled from a subset of the ingested papers, along with the ground-truth source passages for each question. To keep manual curation tractable for a three-person team, an LLM will first generate a larger candidate list of questions and draft answers directly from the Docling-parsed markdown of each paper; the team will then manually review, correct, and filter these down to the final 30–50 pairs, discarding any that are ambiguous, unanswerable from the source text, or too close to a verbatim quote. Because this test set will still be finalized by the same team building the system, there is a risk of confirmation bias, i.e., unintentionally selecting or editing questions that suit the pipeline's known strengths; to mitigate this, question drafting and filtering will be done before final tuning of the retrieval and reranking components is complete, and at least 20% of the final questions will be reviewed by someone outside the immediate development team. The RAGAS framework [5] will run this test set against the pipeline's outputs to compute faithfulness (whether generated claims are supported by retrieved context), context precision (whether retrieved chunks are relevant), context recall (whether all ground-truth facts needed to answer the question were retrieved), and answer relevance scores; these metrics will be computed in a single evaluation pass against the completed core pipeline, so that faithfulness, context precision, context recall, and answer relevance are reported as fixed, comparable scores rather than left unmeasured. To avoid unbudgeted default judge costs and self-preference bias from having Gemini judge its own outputs, RAGAS will be configured with a judge LLM from a different model family/provider than the generation model — e.g., GPT-4o-mini or Claude Haiku — with a documented Gemini fallback if a separate-provider judge is not within budget. To contextualize these scores, the same held-out QA set will also be run against a non-RAG baseline (Gemini 2.5 Flash answering directly from its parametric knowledge, with no retrieved context) using the same RAGAS metrics where applicable (answer relevance) and a manual factual-accuracy check in place of faithfulness, context precision, and context recall, which are undefined without retrieval. This comparison is intended to demonstrate the marginal contribution of the retrieval pipeline rather than evaluate the pipeline in isolation. Time and hardware permitting, this will be extended to an ablation comparing the pipeline with and without the reranker (or the GraphRAG stage, if completed) to quantify that component's contribution, but this ablation is treated as optional rather than a core requirement. As an interim validation step, the core pipeline is first checked with a lightweight manual spot-check against roughly 10–15 QA pairs, run ahead of the full single-configuration RAGAS evaluation described above. # 6. Timeline and Expected Outcomes

The project will be developed across five stages, sequenced so that the core deliverable (Section 5.1) is complete before any extension work begins. Weeks 1–3 focus on ingestion, chunking, and a working dense-retrieval baseline (Docling, ChromaDB). Weeks 4–6 add sparse retrieval, RRF fusion, reranking, and Gemini API integration, including the interim manual spot-check described in Section 5.4, with the core hybrid pipeline complete and independently demoable by the end of week 6. Weeks 7–8 build the Streamlit user interface around the validated pipeline. Week 9 runs the RAGAS evaluation on the completed pipeline together with the non-RAG baseline comparison described in Section 5.4, including the manual factual-accuracy grading the baseline requires; this is expected to take the full week given the added baseline run, with Week 10 available as spillover if grading is not finished in time. Week 10 is reserved as buffer and polish; the Neo4j/GraphRAG layer and the optional RAGAS ablation (Section 5.4) are attempted in this window only if the project remains on schedule, with descoping remaining an option if time or hardware constraints require it. If schedule permits beyond those two, an optional LLM-as-a-judge automated metric for literature reviews (scoring source coverage and synthesis quality) may also be attempted, using a separate-provider judge per the same mitigation described in Section 5.4; this is lower priority than GraphRAG and the RAGAS ablation, and the manual spot-check in Section 5.4 remains the fallback if it is not reached.
The expected outcome is a working AI Research Copilot capable of retrieving and synthesizing information across multiple research papers through a Hybrid Retrieval Engine, presented through an interactive Streamlit interface, with RAGAS-based evaluation results quantifying the system's faithfulness, context precision, context recall, and answer relevance against the curated test set. The Neo4j-based citation graph and any RAGAS ablation are expected outcomes only insofar as the schedule in Section 6 permits; the core pipeline, UI, and single-configuration RAGAS evaluation (including the non-RAG baseline comparison) constitute the guaranteed deliverable.
